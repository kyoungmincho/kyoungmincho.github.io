Kyoungmin Cho Research CV

Research

My research lies primarily in philosophy of action and ethics, and at the intersection of the two. I am especially interested in the purposive structure of intentional action: how actions are organized by ends, how ends figure in practical reasoning and action explanation, and what it is to act for something for its own sake. My work also explores the implications of these questions for agency, practical knowledge, value, practical identity, and happiness.

Papers

The Problem of the Infinite Regress of Ends
Revise and Resubmit, Ratio

Intentional actions are characteristically performed for ends, but explaining one end by appeal to a further end appears to generate a regress. This paper asks what must be true of an end if it is genuinely capable of terminating practical explanation, and develops an account of ultimate ends that does more than identify whatever an agent happens not to pursue for anything further.

Ends in Action: Production, Activity, and Actions Done for Their Own Sake
Under review

What is it to perform an action for its own sake? Drawing on the Aristotelian distinction between kinesis and energeia, I distinguish actions whose ends lie in products or outcomes from actions whose ends are realized in the activity itself, and use that distinction to illuminate the purposive structure of intentional action.

Against Subjectivism about Final Ends: The Argument from Fickleness
Under review

According to subjectivism about final ends, an agent's ultimate ends are fixed by the desires or attitudes she happens to have. I argue that this makes our ultimate ends objectionably fickle: changes in desire would by themselves be sufficient to change what ultimately structures an agent's practical reasoning. The argument suggests that having a final end requires a more substantial relation between agent and end.

On Aristotle's Test for Energeia
Revise and Resubmit, The Review of Metaphysics

In Metaphysics IX.6, Aristotle distinguishes activities from movements by observing that in some cases one can truly say both that one is doing something and that one has done it. I argue that the standard interpretation of this test misconstrues its grammatical basis and offer an alternative account centered on aspect and Aristotle's distinction between energeia and kinesis.
